Environmental Mapping & Geospatial Analysis

Exploring how spatial data can help us understand the relationship between people, places, infrastructure and the environment.

This section presents selected mapping projects developed through academic coursework, independent research and practical fieldwork. The maps explore different applications of Geographic Information Systems (GIS), ranging from urban spatial analysis and land-use planning to climate classification, environmental assessment and water resource management.

My work focuses on using spatial data to understand geographical patterns, assess environmental and development-related challenges, and communicate information in a way that can support planning and decision-making. The projects draw on a range of geospatial datasets and cartographic techniques, with particular attention to the relationship between environmental conditions, infrastructure, land use and human activity.

The maps were developed using QGIS and supporting geospatial datasets. Each project reflects a different application of spatial analysis, as well as the importance of selecting appropriate data, applying suitable analytical methods and presenting results clearly.

01

Fieldwork · Spatial organisation

Spatial Mapping for Property Survey Management

Independent application · June 2026

Map 1 illustrates the use of GIS as a practical tool for organising and monitoring residential property survey activities. Developed independently in June 2026, the map was used during fieldwork to track survey progress across approximately five neighbourhoods. Satellite imagery provided the geographical base, while additional spatial layers highlighted residential properties, road networks, railway infrastructure and the relevant administrative or enumeration area (EA) boundary.

The map was developed to provide a clear spatial reference for field activities, making it easier to distinguish between completed and outstanding survey sections. By displaying individual residential properties alongside the survey grid and neighbourhood boundaries, I could monitor household coverage, identify areas requiring further attention and reduce the likelihood of overlooking sections of the study area.

This application demonstrates the practical value of GIS beyond conventional cartographic presentation. In field-based research, spatial organisation is important for maintaining consistent coverage, managing survey progress and improving the coordination of data collection. The map provided a working reference throughout the survey rather than serving solely as a final visual output.

Demographic geography · Thematic cartography

Population Density and Spatial Distribution in South Africa, 1996

Academic GIS exercise · 1996 census data

Map 2 presents the spatial distribution of population density across South Africa in 1996, using a graduated colour scheme to distinguish areas of varying population concentration. The map incorporates major roads, rivers and capital cities to provide additional geographical context and illustrate the relationship between population distribution and the country's physical and infrastructure networks.

Using data from South Africa's first post-apartheid national census, the map provides a snapshot of population distribution during the early years of democratic governance. It offers an opportunity to examine the geographical concentration of settlement, particularly the differences between densely populated urban and economically active regions and more sparsely populated rural areas.

The representation of population density is particularly useful for understanding the spatial dimensions of development. Population distribution has implications for infrastructure provision, access to public services, transport planning and the allocation of resources. By visualising these patterns geographically, the map provides a foundation for considering how demographic characteristics influence planning priorities across different regions.

The inclusion of major transport routes and rivers adds further context to the analysis, allowing population patterns to be considered alongside accessibility and physical geography. While the map provides a historical representation rather than an explanation of the factors driving settlement patterns, it offers a useful starting point for examining the relationship between population distribution, regional development and spatial inequality in South Africa.

03

Land-use planning · Suitability analysis

Identifying Suitable Areas for Residential Development in Stellenbosch

Applied GIS project · October 2025

Map 3 presents a GIS-based suitability assessment undertaken in October 2025 to identify potential locations for a proposed residential development in Stellenbosch, South Africa. The project was based on a hypothetical developer brief specifying a number of geographical requirements that prospective sites needed to satisfy.

The assessment translated these requirements into spatial criteria. Potential sites had to be located within the Stellenbosch study area, fall within 1 km of a major access road, be situated within 15 km of a high school and have terrain suitable for residential construction. Given the extensive agricultural land surrounding Stellenbosch, the analysis also considered existing farm boundaries to identify properties that could potentially accommodate the proposed development.

GIS was used to bring these different spatial criteria together and narrow down the available land. Rather than evaluating properties individually, the suitability assessment allowed multiple geographical conditions to be considered simultaneously. This resulted in a shortlist of approximately five to six farms that met the initial requirements.

The analysis demonstrates how spatial suitability modelling can translate a development brief into a geographical assessment. By combining accessibility, proximity to essential services and terrain characteristics, GIS can help identify potential development locations while making the selection process more systematic and transparent.

The initial suitability assessment formed the basis for a more detailed examination of the shortlisted properties, particularly in relation to available land area and the specific spatial requirements of the proposed development.

04

Land-use planning · Cartographic communication

Identification of Priority Farms for Residential Development

Applied GIS project · October 2025

Map 4 builds on the preceding suitability assessment by providing a clearer representation of the agricultural properties identified for further consideration. While the previous map focused on the spatial criteria used to determine suitability, this map places greater emphasis on communicating the resulting selection of potential properties.

The map distinguishes between the wider agricultural landscape and the farms that met the initial development requirements through the use of contrasting colours. Selected farms are highlighted in yellow, while the remaining agricultural properties are represented in green. Farm names are also included to make the shortlisted properties easier to identify geographically.

A further consideration in the project was the amount of land required for the proposed development. The intended project footprint was approximately two to three hectares, meaning that the initial identification of suitable farms needed to be followed by a closer examination of available land and terrain within each property. The resulting map therefore served as a reference for identifying potential locations for further investigation and engagement with landowners.

Together, the two maps illustrate how GIS can support different stages of a land-use planning process. The first establishes spatial suitability through the application of defined criteria, while the second communicates the selected properties in a format that is more accessible for interpretation and subsequent decision-making.

The project highlights the value of spatial analysis in early-stage development planning, particularly where decisions involve competing land uses, accessibility requirements and the physical characteristics of available land. It also demonstrates how cartographic design can help translate analytical results into information that can be used by planners, developers and other stakeholders.

05

Climate analysis · Tourism and adaptation

Climate Classification and Tourism Vulnerability in South Africa

Independent project · September 2025

Map 5 explores the geographical distribution of climatic zones across South Africa and considers their relevance to tourism-related climate vulnerability. Developed as an independent project in September 2025, the map formed part of a broader investigation into how changing climatic conditions could influence tourism activities and the environmental systems on which they depend.

The spatial analysis uses the Köppen-Geiger climate classification to illustrate the country's climatic variation. Two tourism destinations were selected as comparative case studies: Waterford Estate in Stellenbosch, representing wine and agricultural tourism, and Sabi Sands Nature Reserve in Mpumalanga, representing safari and wildlife tourism.

The selection of these locations provided an opportunity to examine how different tourism activities are situated within distinct climatic environments. Waterford Estate is located within the Mediterranean climatic region of the Western Cape, where projected climate pressures include rising temperatures, declining rainfall and an increased risk of drought. These conditions have potential implications for water availability, agricultural productivity and the long-term sustainability of wine tourism.

Sabi Sands, situated within the subtropical savanna environment of northeastern South Africa, faces a different set of potential pressures. These include more frequent heat extremes, changes in rainfall patterns and associated stress on biodiversity and wildlife habitats. Such changes could affect ecosystem functioning and the environmental conditions that support safari tourism.

The comparative approach highlights that climate-related risks are not geographically uniform. Different tourism destinations may experience distinct environmental pressures depending on their climatic conditions, ecosystems and dependence on natural resources.

The map therefore provides a geographical foundation for considering climate adaptation within the tourism sector. It also highlights the importance of incorporating regional climatic conditions into environmental planning and tourism policy, particularly where economic activities depend heavily on the continued availability and quality of natural resources.

Hydrology · Environmental monitoring

River Catchment Mapping and Water Quality Monitoring

Academic GIS exercise · Date to be confirmed

Map 6 presents a geographical overview of the Eerste River system in the Western Cape, with particular attention to its catchment area, surrounding terrain and the settlements through which it flows, including Stellenbosch. The map was developed to provide a spatial reference for understanding the river system and informing the selection of potential water quality sampling locations.

The work formed part of an investigation into variations in river water composition along different sections of the river. Understanding the relationship between the river's course, its surrounding landscape and the wider catchment was important when identifying appropriate sampling points. Rather than selecting locations in isolation, the catchment perspective allowed the river to be considered as part of a connected hydrological system.

The intention was to compare water composition at different points along the river, from upstream sections towards its downstream reaches. This approach would help investigate how water quality characteristics vary spatially and provide a basis for considering the influence of surrounding land uses, settlements and other environmental factors.

The inclusion of terrain information adds an important dimension to the map. Topography influences surface runoff and the movement of water through a catchment, making it relevant when examining how surrounding areas contribute to river flows. The identification of major settlements also provides geographical context for considering potential human influences on water quality.

Although the map primarily serves as a geographical overview rather than a detailed hydrological model, it illustrates the value of catchment-based mapping in environmental monitoring. It demonstrates how spatial information can support the design of field investigations and contribute to a more systematic understanding of freshwater systems.

07

Looking across the work

Reflections on Geospatial Analysis and Cartographic Practice

These projects have allowed me to explore the application of GIS across different geographical and environmental contexts. Working with demographic, infrastructure, land-use, climate and hydrological datasets has strengthened my understanding of how spatial information can be used to investigate complex relationships between people, the built environment and natural systems.

A particularly important aspect of this work has been learning to approach mapping as more than a visual exercise. The selection of appropriate datasets, the application of spatial criteria and the interpretation of geographical patterns all influence the conclusions that can be drawn from a map. Projects such as the Stellenbosch residential suitability assessment demonstrated how spatial analysis can contribute to practical planning decisions, while the climate and river mapping exercises highlighted the role of GIS in understanding environmental pressures and supporting resource management.

The projects have also reinforced the importance of cartographic communication. Decisions relating to classification, colour, scale, labelling and the selection of supporting geographical features can significantly influence how spatial information is interpreted. A well-designed map should not only present data accurately but also make the underlying geographical relationships understandable to its intended audience.

Going forward, I am particularly interested in developing these skills further through the integration of GIS with environmental data analysis, sustainability assessment and evidence-based planning. I see geospatial analysis as an important tool for connecting environmental information with practical decisions relating to land use, infrastructure, climate resilience and natural resource management.
